## Key headings/content markers
- <div class="tldr-box"><h2 id="tldr">TL;DR</h2>
- <li>A technical advisor gives opinions. A fractional CTO owns outcomes.</li>
- <li>Advisors typically contribute one to three hours per month. Fractional CTOs commit four to twenty-plus hours per week.</li>
- <li>Advisors rarely have a defined scope or accountability. Fractional CTOs have written 90-day outcomes.</li>
- <li>Cost ranges from $500 to $3,000 per month for advisors and from $4,500 to $25,000 per month for fractional CTOs.</li>
- </div><h2 id="the-core-difference-in-one-paragraph">The core difference in one paragraph</h2>
- <h2 id="what-technical-advisors-actually-do">What technical advisors actually do</h2>
- <p>What advisors typically do:</p>
- <li>Join calls when you invite them</li>
- <li>Review specific documents, proposals, or architectural diagrams</li>
- <li>Give opinions on vendor choices or hiring decisions when asked</li>
- <li>Provide a brand-name reference to investors ("we are advised by...")</li>

I have operated in both shapes across 16 years and more than 250 projects, including a three-week MVP for a Barclays and Bain-backed fintech startup. This guide gives you a clear map.</p>
- <li>Advisors typically contribute one to three hours per month. Fractional CTOs commit four to twenty-plus hours per week.</li>
- <p>A technical advisor is someone you call or email when a decision comes up. A fractional CTO is someone who hunts down the decisions, structures them, and drives the answer without being asked. The difference is ownership. Advisors inform. Fractional CTOs lead.</p>
- <p>What advisors typically do:</p>
- <li>Join calls when you invite them</li>
- <li>Handle crisis situations (the first call goes to them, but they rarely have enough context to help fast)</li>
- <p>The key word across all of those is "owns." A fractional CTO does not need to be invited. They show up on the cadence the scope requires — whether that is daily async updates, a weekly call, or a structured monthly review — and they produce outcomes, not just opinions.</p>
- <li>You have an engineering crisis — something is broken, behind schedule, or being rebuilt for the second time</li>
- <p>A non-technical founder hires an advisor with a strong resume. The advisor gives good answers when asked. But no one is tracking the technical decisions between calls. The development team, often contractors or a small internal team, makes dozens of small choices weekly — which library to use, how to model the database, whether to build a feature or buy a vendor. Each choice individually looks minor. Compounded over six months, they create the codebase that a fractional CTO or a Series A engineer inherits and has to partly rebuild.</p>
- <p>Advisors typically cost between $500 and $3,000 per month, often partly or fully in equity. The equity component ranges from 0.1 percent to 0.5 percent on a four-year vest.</p>
- <p>Yes, and it sometimes happens organically. If an advisor proves useful over three to six months and the company's needs grow, the relationship can be restructured to a fractional scope with a real retainer and real deliverables. The risk is that both sides get comfortable with the lighter commitment and never make the formal shift, even when the company needs more.</p>
